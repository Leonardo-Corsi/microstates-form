## Methods

### A. Before microstates: GFP and topographic dissimilarity

We first characterized the relationship between global field power (GFP) and adjacent-sample topographic dissimilarity (TD), following the logic of previous work showing that high field-strength EEG samples tend to coincide with more stable scalp topographies (Zanesco 2020). GFP was used as a measure of instantaneous electric field strength. TD was used as a measure of change in scalp-field configuration between consecutive samples after removing field strength.

For each EEG sample $x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{ch}}$, the map is average-referenced and normalized by the GFP:

$$
x_t^c=x_t-\bar{x}*t\mathbf{1}, \ \
GFP_t=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N*{ch}}
\sum_i
(x_{t,i}^c)^2
}
\ \rarr \
z_t=\frac{x_t^c}{GFP_t}
$$

and adjacent topographic dissimilarity is computed as:

$$
TD_t=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N_{ch}}
\sum_i
(z_{t,i}-z_{t-1,i})^2
}
$$

This quantity measures changes in topographic configuration independently of instantaneous field strength. The expected empirical pattern is not a deterministic one-to-one dependency, but a constrained joint distribution: high GFP should compress TD toward low values, whereas low GFP may allow a broad range of TD. We therefore represented the GFP-TD relationship using both linear and log-log density plots, as well as GFP-binned summaries of TD. This analysis served as the baseline against which MEED-derived quantities were evaluated.

### B. Microstate-equivalent electric dipoles

The MEED representation was introduced as a compact model-based description of scalp topographies. The motivation is that canonical microstate maps are usually smooth radial or approximately linear gradients across the scalp. We do not interpret these maps as literal single-source dipoles, which would oversimplify EEG dynamics. Instead, following the same practical assumption of microstate analysis that most of the relevant variance is expressed by smooth voltage topographies, we use an electric dipole as a low-dimensional descriptor of scalp-field geometry.

For MEED fitting, each microstate map $b_m\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{ch}}$ is average-referenced and L2-normalized:

$$
\tilde b_m =
\frac{
b_m-\bar b_m\mathbf{1}
}{
|b_m-\bar b_m\mathbf{1}|^2
},
\ \
\tilde B=
\begin{bmatrix}
\tilde b_1 & \vert
\tilde b_2 & \vert & 
\cdots & \vert & \tilde b_M\
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{ch}\times M}
$$

This differs from the GFP-normalized continuous samples above only in the final scale convention: GFP normalization gives unit RMS maps, whereas MEED fitting uses unit L2-norm maps. This makes projection energy directly interpretable as a fraction of normalized topographic energy.

The dipole model starts from the potential $V_i$ generated by an electric dipole with moment $p$ located at $r_0$ at a sensor position $r_i$:

$$
V_i(r_0,p) = V(r_0,p)\biggr\rvert_{r_i}
= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\cdot
\frac{
p^\top (r_i-r_0)
}{
|r_i-r_0|^3
}
$$

In the full dipole model, both $r_0$ and $p$ are unknown. Fitting the model therefore requires a nonlinear optimization over dipole location, with a linear moment estimate conditional on each tested location. This full model is flexible, but it also uses more degrees of freedom and may fit noncanonical or noisy topographies too optimistically.

The fixed MEED model simplifies the problem by fixing the dipole at the center of the fitted head sphere. Let the fitted sphere have center $c$ and radius $R$. Electrode coordinates are first expressed relative to this fitted sphere and then projected to the unit sphere:

$$
u_i=\frac{r_i-c}{R}, \ \
s_i=\frac{u_i}{|u_i|}, \ \
S=
\begin{bmatrix}
s_1^\top\
\vdots\
s_{N_{ch}}^\top
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{ch}\times 3}
$$

Because the analyzed scalp maps are average-referenced, the coordinate basis is also channel-centered before fitting:

$$
C=I-\frac{1}{N_{ch}}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top, \ \
S_c=CS
$$

This operation does not redefine the physical head center. It only ensures that the model topographies lie in the same average-referenced sensor space as the data.

For a dipole fixed at the sphere center, the idealized field on the unit sphere is linear in the dipole moment:

$$
\hat b = S_c p
$$

However, the columns of $S_c$ are not exactly orthonormal, because of nonuniform electrode placement and deviations from ideal spherical geometry. We therefore define the electrode-geometry matrix $M_S=S_c^\top S_c$ and orthonormalize the fixed-dipole scaffold as:

$$
S_{\text{orth}}=S_cM_S^{-1/2}, \ \
S_{\text{orth}}^\top S_{\text{orth}}=I
$$

The fixed MEED projection is then:

$$
Q=S_{\text{orth}}^\top\tilde B, \ \
\hat B_{\text{proj}}=S_{\text{orth}}Q
$$

where $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times M}$ contains the fixed-dipole projection coordinates and $\hat B_{\text{proj}}$ is the orthogonal projection of the maps onto the fixed-MEED subspace.

For each map $m$, with $q_m$ the corresponding column of $Q$, we write:

$$
q_m=\rho_m v_m, \ \
\rho_m=|q_m|, \ \
v_m=\frac{q_m}{|q_m|}
$$

Since each $\tilde b_m$ has unit L2 norm and $S_{\text{orth}}$ is orthonormal, $\rho_m^2=|q_m|^2$ is the fraction of normalized topographic energy captured by the fixed-MEED scaffold. We call this quantity projection $R^2$ or dipolarity. It is a diagnostic of model compatibility, not the retained MEED coordinate.

The retained fixed MEED descriptor is the direction $v_m$, expressed by two angular coordinates after polarity canonicalization:

$$
v_m \rightarrow D_m=(\theta_m,\phi_m)
$$

where $\theta_m$ is the anatomical azimuth and $\phi_m$ is the elevation. Since scalp topographies are treated up to polarity, $v_m$ and $-v_m$ are considered equivalent and the sign is chosen before extracting the angles. The corresponding unit fixed-MEED template is:

$$
\hat b_m^{MEED}=S_{\text{orth}}v_m
$$

This reconstruction uses only the angular descriptor and does not retain projection strength. Its reconstruction quality was evaluated using polarity-invariant $R^2$, after aligning the sign of the reconstructed map to the original map.

The fixed model was considered as the primary MEED representation, while the full model was used as a diagnostic comparison, since it can improve reconstruction quality, but it uses additional parameters and may fit noncanonical or noisy topographies too optimistically. The method-development analysis tested two questions to establish whether $\rho^2$ can act as a meaningful measure of microstate-like dipolarity rather than merely reproducing arbitrary map variance, and compared the results for the fixed and full dipole models:

* first, literature meta-microstate maps were fitted using fixed and full dipole models to determine whether the fixed model preserved most of the relevant topographic structure while retaining interpretability.
* second, synthetic/null maps were used to test whether fixed MEED behaved as a constrained field descriptor, where the expected behavior was that iid spatial noise and high-order spatial structure should show low fixed-MEED fit, whereas low-order smooth gradients should show higher fit.


### C. Main properties of MEED fitting on real data

The real-data analysis used MEED as an extension of the GFP-TD framework. In classical microstate analysis, GFP is often used to identify samples whose maps are expected to be more reliable for clustering or interpretation. This reliability has usually been understood in terms of topographic stability: high-GFP samples tend to show lower adjacent-sample TD. We extended this idea by asking whether reliable samples are not only stable, but also dipole-like in the MEED sense.

For each sample, we computed three primary quantities: $GFP_t$ as field strength, $TD_t$ as full adjacent topographic instability, and $\rho_t^2$ as fixed-MEED dipolarity. The first real-data hypothesis was that the known GFP-TD relationship should be accompanied by a GFP-$\rho^2$ relationship. Specifically, high-GFP samples should tend to show low TD and high MEED dipolarity. This would indicate that high field-strength moments are not only topographically stable, but are also well described by the smooth radial or linear field family captured by the fixed MEED model. Conversely, if high-GFP samples were stable but showed low or highly variable $\rho^2$, then MEED would not capture the same stable topographic regime emphasized by the GFP literature.

The second real-data question concerned the structure of TD itself. TD measures the full change in normalized scalp topography, but it does not say whether that change is due to a change in the dipole-like part of the field or to a change outside the MEED model. MEED allows this decomposition because the fixed-MEED scaffold defines an orthonormal subspace of the average-referenced sensor space.

For each normalized scalp map $z_t$, the fixed-MEED projection coordinates, projected component, and residual component are:

$$
q_t=S_{\mathrm{orth}}^\top z_t, \ \
p_t=S_{\mathrm{orth}}q_t, \ \
e_t=z_t-p_t, \ \
z_t=p_t+e_t
$$

where $S_{\mathrm{orth}}^\top S_{\mathrm{orth}}=I$. The MEED-projected component of adjacent topographic dissimilarity is therefore:

$$
TD_{\mathrm{MEED,proj}}(t)=|p_t-p_{t-1}|
=|q_t-q_{t-1}|
$$

where the equality follows from the orthonormality of $S_{\mathrm{orth}}$. This quantity measures the part of adjacent topographic change that lies inside the fixed-MEED subspace.

Using the polar decomposition $q_t=\rho_tu_t$, with $\rho_t=|q_t|$ and $|u_t|=1$, the projected MEED change can be decomposed into a radial component and an angular component:

$$
|q_t-q_{t-1}|^2=
(\rho_t-\rho_{t-1})^2
+
2\rho_t\rho_{t-1}(1-u_t^\top u_{t-1})
$$

The first term defines the dipolarity-change component:

$$
TD_{\rho}(t)=|\rho_t-\rho_{t-1}|
$$

This measures whether the scalp map becomes more or less compatible with the fixed-MEED scaffold from one sample to the next.

The second term defines the MEED angular component:

$$
TD_{\mathrm{MEED,ang}}(t)=
\sqrt{
2\rho_t\rho_{t-1}(1-u_t^\top u_{t-1})
}
$$

This measures the component of adjacent topographic change that is due to a change in fixed-dipole direction. Unlike a raw angular velocity such as $\arccos(u_t^\top u_{t-1})/\Delta t$, this term is expressed in the same squared-distance geometry as TD and is weighted by the dipolarity of both adjacent samples. Thus, angular changes contribute strongly only when both adjacent maps are well represented by the fixed-MEED scaffold.

The residual component is:

$$
TD_{\mathrm{residual}}(t)=|e_t-e_{t-1}|
$$

which measures adjacent topographic change outside the fixed-MEED subspace. Since the MEED projection and residual lie in orthogonal subspaces, the squared topographic change decomposes as:

$$
|z_t-z_{t-1}|^2=
TD_{\mathrm{MEED,proj}}^2(t)
+
TD_{\mathrm{residual}}^2(t)=
TD_{\rho}^2(t)
+
TD_{\mathrm{MEED,ang}}^2(t)
+
TD_{\mathrm{residual}}^2(t)
$$

If TD is reported as root-mean-square channel distance, as in the GFP-TD analysis, the same decomposition holds after applying the same $1/\sqrt{N_{ch}}$ scaling to each distance term. Thus, the decomposition is a geometric partition of adjacent topographic change into dipolarity change, dipole-direction change, and non-MEED residual change.

This decomposition was used to test whether MEED adds information beyond the GFP-TD relationship. The practical analyses were organized as follows. First, we tested the classical relation between GFP and TD. Second, we tested whether GFP also predicts MEED dipolarity $\rho^2$. Third, we tested whether TD is primarily explained by MEED-projected change or by residual non-MEED change. Fourth, we tested whether the MEED analogue of the GFP-TD relation holds, namely whether high $\rho^2$ constrains $TD_{\mathrm{MEED,ang}}$ toward low values.

In this framework, the most interpretable regime is:

$$
\text{high GFP} + \text{low TD} + \text{high } \rho^2 + \text{low } TD_{\mathrm{MEED,ang}}
$$

This indicates that the scalp field is strong, stable, dipole-like, and stable in its fixed-MEED direction. In contrast, high GFP with low TD but low $\rho^2$ would indicate a strong and stable field that is not well described by the fixed-MEED scaffold. High $\rho^2$ with high $TD_{\mathrm{MEED,ang}}$ would indicate a dipole-like field undergoing rapid directional change. High residual dissimilarity would indicate that adjacent topographic instability is dominated by components outside the fixed-MEED model.

Thus, MEED does not replace GFP, TD, or canonical microstate labels. Instead, it separates field strength, full topographic stability, dipolarity, dipole-direction stability, and residual non-MEED instability into distinct measurable quantities.
