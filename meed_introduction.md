## Introduction

EEG microstate analysis describes the continuous scalp electric field as a sequence of brief periods during which the voltage topography remains approximately stable. The classical framework is therefore topographic before it is temporal: duration, occurrence, coverage, and transition probabilities are interpretable only if the maps being assigned are themselves credible, comparable across subjects, and sufficiently close to the template space used for backfitting. This point has become increasingly important as microstate analysis has moved from descriptive resting-state work to clinical and methodological applications in which small differences in temporal metrics may be interpreted as evidence for altered brain dynamics.

The operational basis of the field remains strong. Microstate segmentation was formalized as a topographic clustering problem in which scalp maps are treated up to polarity, because a sign inversion changes field orientation but not the spatial configuration of the underlying voltage pattern (Pascual-Marqui et al., 1995). The canonical resting-state tradition was consolidated by large normative work showing reproducible classes across age, and subsequent reviews have summarized the evidence that a few quasi-stable topographies can be reliably identified and related to large-scale brain network dynamics (Koenig et al., 2002; Michel & Koenig, 2018). At the same time, this tradition creates a methodological dependency: once a continuous EEG recording is reduced to a symbolic sequence of labels, the reliability of downstream metrics depends on how well each instantaneous map supports its assigned label.

Global field power (GFP) has historically provided the main practical heuristic for selecting informative samples. High-GFP samples tend to be more stable in topographic configuration than adjacent low-GFP samples, and this empirical relationship supports the common practice of fitting microstates at GFP peaks (Zanesco, 2020). However, GFP measures field strength, not field geometry. A strong scalp field may be stable without being close to a canonical microstate map, and it may be close to a template without being clearly separable from its nearest competitor. Topographic dissimilarity (TD or DISS) partially addresses this problem by measuring changes in normalized scalp configuration independently of field strength, but it remains a full sensor-space distance. It does not specify whether a change reflects movement within a smooth canonical field family, displacement away from that family, or ambiguity between structurally similar templates.

This distinction matters because recent methodological work has shown that microstate labels are not automatically exchangeable across studies, groups, or fitting regimes. Meta-microstate work has made explicit that maps assigned to the same canonical class can vary substantially across studies, motivating more objective comparison with reference templates rather than unverified use of A-D labels (Koenig et al., 2023). Group-level simulations and empirical analyses have also shown that using subgroup-specific maps can inflate false positive temporal effects when no true group difference exists, while using a single common map set can confound genuine topographic differences with temporal metrics (Murphy et al., 2024). Reliability studies further indicate that fitting choices, template level, transition estimation, and electrode density affect the stability of microstate measures (Kleinert et al., 2024). These findings do not invalidate microstate analysis. They show that temporal interpretation requires an explicit validation layer for template comparability and assignment credibility.

A second limitation is the hard-label structure of standard backfitting. Winner-takes-all assignment converts a continuous vector of template similarities into one discrete state. This is useful for computing conventional temporal metrics, but it hides several different forms of uncertainty. A sample may have low similarity to all templates, high similarity to two competing templates, or high similarity to a winning template that is itself structurally correlated with the runner-up. These situations have different interpretations but can produce the same final label. The problem has already been recognized in probabilistic microstate work, where deterministic assignment was replaced by fuzzy clustering and probabilistic labels to retain information about uncertainty (Dinov & Leech, 2017). It also appears in geometric analyses showing that microstate distance distributions overlap and that transitions may be better viewed as continuous trajectories than as purely discrete jumps (von Wegner & Laufs, 2019). More recent polarity-sensitive manifold work reinforces the same general point: the conventional polarity-invariant label sequence is only one projection of richer electric-field dynamics (Kashihara et al., 2025).

The source-modelling literature provides a useful but easily misinterpreted adjacent idea. Equivalent current dipoles have long been used as compact descriptors of scalp field patterns, and ICA studies often quantify the residual variance of component maps after fitting a single equivalent dipole (Delorme et al., 2012). In that setting, dipolarity is used as a practical marker of whether a scalp map is compatible with a simple, coherent field pattern. This does not imply that every EEG map, or every microstate, is generated by one literal focal source. Microstate source-imaging and EEG-fMRI studies instead support a network-level interpretation: surface microstate classes are associated with distributed and partly overlapping cortical networks, and their topographies can be shaped by the spatial distribution of alpha-band intracortical sources (Britz et al., 2010; Custo et al., 2017; Milz et al., 2017). Therefore, any dipole-based extension of microstate analysis must be framed as a field-level representation of scalp geometry, not as source localization.

The microstate-equivalent electric dipole (MEED) is introduced from this restricted position. MEED does not claim that a microstate is a single neural dipole. It uses a fixed, scalp-centred dipole scaffold as a low-dimensional geometric basis for describing smooth voltage topographies. Each centered and normalized scalp map is projected into this scaffold, yielding an angular direction that summarizes the main field geometry and a projection strength, or $\rho^2$, that quantifies how much of the normalized map is captured by the MEED subspace. In this sense, MEED separates quantities that are often conflated in standard pipelines: GFP indexes field strength, template correlation indexes class proximity, and $\rho^2$ indexes compatibility with a smooth dipole-like field descriptor.

Preliminary analyses motivating this development showed that the fixed MEED scaffold can capture the geometry of meta-microstate solutions with high explained variance, while preserving interpretable angular structure. Lateralized maps are organized mainly as left-right gradients, whereas frontal and posterior radial configurations separate along a different angular component. In resting-state LEMON recordings, MEED-space distance reproduced the classical GFP-stability relationship observed for TD, and projection strength explained substantially more variance in winning-template similarity than GFP (Babayan et al., 2019). This suggests that MEED is not merely another way to measure amplitude. It may index whether an instantaneous scalp map is shaped like a plausible canonical field and whether its backfitted label is geometrically credible.

The methodological opportunity is therefore not to replace standard microstates, but to make their use more auditable. At the template level, MEED can characterize whether subject-level or study-level maps occupy the expected angular positions of reference meta-microstates, and whether apparent A-D labels correspond to comparable field geometries. At the sample level, MEED can identify moments that are strong but geometrically noncanonical, canonical-like but low-amplitude, or ambiguous in correlation space but separable along a specific angular axis. At the sequence level, MEED can support uncertainty-aware cleaning of symbolic labels by marking transitions driven by residual non-MEED instability rather than by coherent movement through the canonical field space.

A further development is continuous microstate analysis. Conventional temporal metrics require a discrete label sequence, but the underlying EEG trajectory evolves in a high-dimensional sensor space. MEED offers a constrained phase-space representation in which this trajectory can be studied through angular displacement, angular velocity, dipolarity changes, and residual instability. This is especially relevant for transitions between maps that are correlated in sensor space but distinct in MEED geometry, such as cases where the winner and runner-up differ mainly along sagittal or transverse components. Instead of asking only whether the label changed from C to D, MEED permits the more specific question of whether the scalp field moved coherently through the angular sector separating those templates, or whether the apparent transition was produced by low-confidence residual fluctuations.

The method section formalizes this proposal. It first anchors MEED to the established GFP-TD framework, then defines the fixed dipole scaffold, projection coordinates, angular descriptors, and projection strength. It then uses the orthogonality of the fixed MEED scaffold to decompose adjacent topographic change into MEED-projected, radial, angular, and residual components. The purpose of this decomposition is methodological: to determine whether stable high-GFP periods are also dipole-like and directionally stable, whether template proximity is better predicted by field geometry than by amplitude alone, and whether assignment credibility can be estimated before relying on the resulting microstate sequence.

The expected future role of MEED is as a standardization and validation layer for microstate workflows. A complete workflow should not only report duration, occurrence, coverage, transition probabilities, and global explained variance. It should also report whether the fitted maps align with reference meta-templates, whether instantaneous labels have sufficient winner strength and margin, whether ambiguity is driven by structural similarity among templates, and whether the assigned maps lie in a plausible smooth-field subspace. MEED contributes to this agenda by adding an interpretable geometry-corrected descriptor that can be compared across montages, datasets, fitting regimes, and clinical groups. Its value will depend on showing non-redundancy with GFP, TD/DISS, template correlation, and probabilistic assignment confidence, and on testing whether MEED-derived reliability measures improve the robustness of temporal and transition metrics.
